The Quiet Science of Sourdough

By Mara Lindqvist · Published March 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Every sourdough loaf begins with something you cannot see: a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria that has been fed nothing but flour and water. Commercial yeast is a single, fast and predictable strain. A starter is a small ecosystem, and like every ecosystem it rewards patience more than precision.

The good news is that you do not need a laboratory to bake great bread. You need to understand three things – what lives in your jar, how water changes your dough, and why time is your most important ingredient.

What actually lives in a starter

A mature starter is dominated by lactic acid bacteria, which outnumber the yeast by roughly a hundred to one. The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acid, which give the bread its tang and help it stay fresh for days. The yeast produce carbon dioxide, which is what makes the dough rise.

The two groups cooperate surprisingly well. The acids keep unwanted microbes away, while the yeast tolerate the sour environment that would stop most other organisms. When people say their starter "smells like nail polish", they are usually noticing ethyl acetate – a harmless sign that the culture is hungry and needs to be fed.

Five habits of reliable bakers

  • Feed the starter at the same time every day and use it when it has roughly doubled.
  • Weigh everything, including the water – cups and spoons are far too imprecise.
  • Keep notes on room temperature, because a few degrees can change the timing by hours.
  • Watch the dough instead of the clock: look for a domed surface and visible bubbles.
  • Let the bread cool for at least an hour before slicing, or the crumb will turn gummy.

Timing is a variable, not a rule

Most recipes give you times, but those times only hold at the temperature the author happened to have in their kitchen. At 24 °C a bulk fermentation might take five hours; at 19 °C the same dough can need eight. A cold proof in the refrigerator slows everything down even further, which is exactly why so many bakers shape in the evening and bake the next morning.

"Recipes tell you what happened in someone else's kitchen. Your dough tells you what is happening in yours – learn to listen to it."

– Jonas Okafor, head baker at a small neighbourhood bakery

A simple weekend schedule

  1. Friday, 9 pm: feed the starter and leave it on the counter overnight.
  2. Saturday, 9 am: mix flour and water and let the mixture rest for an hour.
  3. Saturday, 10 am: add starter and salt, then stretch and fold every 30 minutes.
  4. Saturday, 3 pm: shape the loaf and move it into the refrigerator.
  5. Sunday, 8 am: bake straight from the fridge in a preheated pot.

Doing the math

Bakers express every ingredient as a percentage of the total flour weight, the so-called baker's percentages. If you want to scale a recipe, change flour_g and keep the ratios. Press Ctrl + C to copy the snippet below into your own notes:

def bakers_percentages(flour_g, water_g, starter_g, salt_g):
    """Return each ingredient as a percentage of the flour weight."""
    return {
        "water": round(water_g / flour_g * 100, 1),
        "starter": round(starter_g / flour_g * 100, 1),
        "salt": round(salt_g / flour_g * 100, 1),
    }

print(bakers_percentages(500, 375, 100, 10))
# {'water': 75.0, 'starter': 20.0, 'salt': 2.0}

If you want to dig deeper into the history and the microbiology, the Wikipedia article on sourdough is a good starting point. Whatever you read, remember that the best teacher is a loaf you bake this weekend and the notes you take while doing it.
